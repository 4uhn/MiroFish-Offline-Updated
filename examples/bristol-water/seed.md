# Water Contamination Crisis in Bristol, England (April 2026)

## Overview

Bristol Water has issued an emergency "Do Not Drink" notice for approximately 45,000 households in the BS5, BS6, and BS7 postcode areas of Bristol after elevated levels of lead and industrial solvents were detected in the water supply. The contamination is believed to originate from a legacy industrial site adjacent to the Purdown water treatment works, where construction activity disturbed sealed waste containment.

## Timeline

- **14 April 2026 (Monday)**: Routine sampling at Purdown treatment works flags anomalous readings. Internal investigation begins.
- **15 April (Tuesday)**: Follow-up samples confirm lead levels at 3x the regulatory limit and traces of trichloroethylene (TCE) in treated water leaving the plant. Bristol Water notifies the Drinking Water Inspectorate (DWI).
- **16 April (Wednesday, 6am)**: "Do Not Drink" notice issued via text alert, social media, and local radio for BS5, BS6, and BS7 postcodes. Bottled water distribution points established at 8 locations.
- **16 April (Wednesday, 2pm)**: Bristol City Council declares a Major Incident. Schools in the affected area close early. NHS Bristol advises pregnant women and children under 5 to attend GP surgeries for blood lead level testing.
- **17 April (Thursday)**: Environmental Agency confirms the source: a breached containment cell at the former Eastville Chemical Works site, disturbed by groundwork for a new housing development (Persimmon Homes). Persimmon suspends all works.
- **18 April (Friday)**: DWI issues an Enforcement Notice against Bristol Water. Lead levels begin declining but remain above limits. 12 children under 5 found to have elevated blood lead levels. Public anger intensifies.
- **19 April (Saturday)**: Community protest at College Green (est. 2,000 people). Bristol Water CEO issues public apology and announces free water quality testing for all affected homes. The Secretary of State for Environment orders an independent review.

## The Contaminants

### Lead
Lead at 30 µg/L detected (regulatory limit: 10 µg/L). Lead is a neurotoxin with no safe level of exposure for children. Even short-term elevated exposure can cause developmental harm in children under 5. Adults may experience fatigue, joint pain, and cognitive effects.

### Trichloroethylene (TCE)
TCE at 15 µg/L detected (regulatory limit: 10 µg/L). TCE is an industrial degreasing solvent classified as a Group 1 carcinogen. Short-term exposure at these levels is unlikely to cause acute harm, but has prompted precautionary health advice.

## Affected Population

- 45,000 households (~120,000 residents) in BS5 (Easton, St George), BS6 (Redland, Cotham, Montpelier), and BS7 (Bishopston, Horfield, Lockleaze)
- Approximately 8,000 children under 10 in the affected area
- 47 schools and nurseries issued closure or "no tap water" guidance
- University of the West of England (UWE) Frenchay campus partially affected
- Local businesses, cafes, and restaurants forced to close or use bottled water

## Key Stakeholders

- **Drinking Water Inspectorate (DWI)**: Regulatory body overseeing water quality.
- **Bristol City Council**: Declared Major Incident. Coordinating emergency response and bottled water logistics.
- **Environment Agency**: Investigating the contamination source and Persimmon Homes' liability.
- **Persimmon Homes**: Developer whose groundwork breached the containment. Suspended operations.
- **NHS Bristol, North Somerset and South Gloucestershire ICB**: Health response coordination.

## Context

Bristol Water supplies 1.2 million customers across the Bristol region. The Purdown treatment works handles approximately 40% of the city's supply. The former Eastville Chemical Works operated from 1945 to 1987 and processed industrial solvents. It was remediated in 1994 under less stringent standards than current regulations require. Local campaigners have repeatedly raised concerns about the adequacy of the containment, most recently in a 2019 petition to Bristol City Council that received 3,400 signatures.

The incident has drawn national attention due to the scale of population affected, the involvement of children, and questions about regulatory oversight of legacy contamination sites adjacent to critical water infrastructure.
